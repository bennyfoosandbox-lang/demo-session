1. NUS launches "AI Sense Maker," an AI chatbot to unlock 150,000 rare collections and research papers

NUS unveiled AI Sense Maker on 28 Jul, an AI research chatbot built on OpenAI's GPT-4.1 with retrieval-augmented generation, drawing on a 150,000-item repository of digitised rare collections and internal research papers spanning engineering, life sciences, medicine, humanities and computing. University librarian Associate Professor Natalie Pang said the goal is to cut research-discovery time from roughly two weeks to about 30 minutes, while guard rails keep the tool from pulling answers off the open internet so every response stays cited to trusted sources. Highlights in its training set include Lat Pau, Singapore's first Chinese-language daily (1881), and the 1817 first edition of Stamford Raffles' The History of Java. The tool launches for students, faculty and alumni in August, with chat-memory and voice-to-text features planned next — a concrete, well-explained example of NUS positioning itself as a careful, credibility-first adopter of AI in research and teaching rather than a reflexive one.

Sources: The Straits Times

2. NUS climbs to 5th globally in AI in new US News rankings — Singapore the only Southeast Asian nation with two universities in the world top 10

The 2026–2027 US News Best Global Universities subject rankings, evaluating 2,250 institutions worldwide, placed NUS 5th globally for artificial intelligence (up four spots) alongside NTU at 2nd, making Singapore the only Southeast Asian country with two universities in the AI world top 10; Malaysia was the region's only other entrant, with four universities ranked 165th–241st. China dominated the list with eight of the top ten, led by Tsinghua at No. 1, and no US or European university made the cut. Beyond AI, NUS also ranked 1st globally in optics, 3rd in chemistry, 5th in materials science and 6th in computer science, while its overall global rank rose to 16th (2nd in Asia). Coming days after NUS's newsroom trumpeted its mathematics ranking, this is another data point in a strong July for NUS's global-rankings narrative, even as NTU continues to edge it out specifically in AI and computer science.

Sources: VnExpress International, US News Best Global Universities

3. NUS engineers unveil X-Sig, a single skin patch that fuses heart and muscle signals for simpler wearable health monitoring

A team led by Assistant Professor Liu Yuxin at NUS's College of Design and Engineering published X-Sig in Nature Sensors on 28 Jul: a cross-modal epidermal sensor that stacks a conductive electrode and an ultrathin piezoelectric film into one patch, fusing electrical signals (ECG, EMG) with mechanical ones (pulse waves, force myography) into a single output channel instead of requiring separate sensors and circuitry for each. Worn on the wrist, it estimated blood pressure continuously and cufflessly with mean errors of just 0.27 mmHg systolic and 0.33 mmHg diastolic against a standard cuff monitor — meeting the highest IEEE accuracy grade — and on the forearm it classified 10 hand gestures at 96.4% accuracy, well above single-signal approaches, using far fewer training samples. No skin irritation was observed after 24 hours of continuous wear, and the electrode materials can be fully recycled with water and ethanol. It's a solid, internationally publishable engineering result that reinforces NUS's steady output in wearable health-tech.

Sources: Medical Xpress, EurekAlert!

4. NUS unveils a lightbulb-inspired reactor tackling hydrogen production, plastics recycling and methane conversion

Professor Yan Ning, Director of the NUS Centre for Hydrogen and Carbon Innovations, and his team detailed a reactor that heats a thin metal filament — the same principle behind an incandescent lightbulb — to over 1,200°C to drive industrial chemical reactions efficiently on electricity, keeping the surrounding system cool and compact. Published across three separate papers (Nature Chemical Engineering, October 2025; Nature Communications, November 2025; and Nature Sustainability, April 2026), the platform achieved near-complete ammonia-to-hydrogen conversion in a markedly smaller footprint than conventional systems, broke down common plastics like polyethylene and polypropylene into reusable chemical building blocks, and converted methane into valuable manufacturing chemicals while co-producing hydrogen. The team is now working with an industry partner to explore ammonia-based energy applications and is looking to scale the platform for industrial use — a compact but credible sustainability and decarbonisation story for NUS's engineering research pipeline.

Sources: NUS News

5. "A doctorate in determination": NUS graduate Dr Mervin Tee's journey from failing O-Levels to a PhD, alongside his wife

NUS News and The Straits Times profiled Dr Mervin Tee, 37, who at 16 failed his O-Levels and spent 10 months working 12-hour shifts in an elevator-parts factory for S$3.50 an hour to fund a retake, before working his way through Republic Polytechnic and a Newcastle University/SIT scholarship, five years in social work, and ultimately a PhD in cognitive decline and neuroimaging at the NUS Saw Swee Hock School of Public Health. He accelerated his doctorate to graduate this year alongside his wife, Dr Cindy Chan, a fellow SSHSPH researcher studying children's nutrition — the couple welcomed their first child during the final year of both PhDs. The story, part of NUS News's Commencement 2026 series, is a low-key but effective piece of the university's "second-chances" narrative, humanising its public-health graduate programme through a genuinely unusual personal arc rather than an institutional achievement.

Sources: NUS News, The Straits Times
