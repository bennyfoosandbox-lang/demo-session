1. Hostel fees rise up to S$80 a month at NUS, NTU and SMU for the new academic year

Ahead of orientation week (3–8 Aug) and the AY2026/27 semester start, NUS confirmed hostel fee increases across its halls: a non-air-conditioned single room at Eusoff Hall, King Edward VII Hall and Temasek Hall now costs S$165 a week, up S$14 from S$151, while an air-conditioned twin-sharing UTown Residence room rose S$20.50 to S$275.50 per person per week. NTU and SMU raised their own hostel rates in parallel, with SMU's increases only taking effect from AY2027/28. The Straits Times' print coverage frames this as a recurring, multi-year pattern of hikes tied to rising operating costs, landing just as thousands of new and returning students move onto campus — a pocketbook story with direct bearing on student sentiment even though it generated no visible public backlash in this cycle.

Sources: Mothership, The Straits Times (Magzter reprint)

2. "Starting out and up": three more Class of 2026 teams turn NUS projects into start-ups

The piece continues NUS's steady post-Commencement genre of framing its Class of 2026 as founders rather than just job-seekers.

4. NUS spintronics team out-solves tested quantum annealers on optimisation problems, using randomness as a resource

Prof Yang Hyunsoo's team in the NUS Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering published two Nature Communications papers describing a "probabilistic spintronic processor" — 144 compact magnetic-tunnel-junction random-number generators wired in parallel to solve quadratic assignment (optimisation) problems. Tested against quadratic assignment problems, the chip delivered a 3.2-times speedup and 58.3% energy savings over an equivalent CPU implementation, and produced consistently feasible solutions where the D-Wave quantum annealers benchmarked against it struggled as problem size grew. First author Yang Shuhan, a PhD student in the College of Design and Engineering, described the approach as treating randomness "as a computing resource" rather than a source of error — a notable applied-hardware result, though the underlying research announcement dates to early July, so this item is carried as a fallback item outside the usual 24–48h window.

Sources: NUS Newsroom, EurekAlert!, Tech Xplore

5. NUS FinTech Lab student builds "Verix," a blockchain trust layer for AI agents, at an XRPL residency in London

Sumit Sanjay Shinde, an NUS School of Computing student affiliated with the NUS FinTech Lab (led by Prof Jungpil Hahn), was one of just 14 university students worldwide selected for Ripple's XRPL Student Builder Residency, a four-week programme in London. There he built Verix, a blockchain-based settlement system built on the XRP Ledger that combines on-chain identity, native escrow, cryptographic verification and immutable reputation records so that autonomous AI agents can pay each other only after a task outcome is verified — addressing a gap in payment infrastructure that Shinde frames as "the trust layer for the agentic economy." It is a small story by itself, but part of a recurring pattern this year of individual NUS Computing students landing selective, industry-run residencies and returning with shipped prototypes.

Sources: NUS FinTech Lab
