1. Korea's regulatory science centre signs MOU with NUS Medicine to build an Asia-wide biohealth network

The Korea Regulatory Science Center (K-RSC), headed by Director General Oh Jae-ho, signed a Memorandum of Understanding on 29 July with NUS's Yong Loo Lin School of Medicine, represented by Assoc Prof Seok Kwon Soga of the Department of Pharmacology. The pact commits both sides to co-host conferences and symposia, exchange research findings and case studies on regulatory developments across ASEAN, run joint academic and policy research, and help build out a regional regulatory-science collaboration hub. It extends NUS Medicine's push to position itself as a bridge for biohealth policy expertise between Northeast and Southeast Asia, alongside its existing Duke-NUS Centre of Regulatory Excellence work. No financial terms were disclosed.

Sources: BioSpectrum Asia

2. Singapore's National Precision Medicine programme publishes its Phase II results in Nature Genetics

A paper published 29 July in Nature Genetics, co-authored by NUS-affiliated researchers among the national NPM consortium, details how the programme's second phase (2020–2025) sequenced the genomes of 100,000 Singaporeans to build the PRECISE-SG100K dataset, the country's first large-scale, multi-ancestry Asian genomic resource. The paper highlights the translation of findings into mainstream care, using familial hypercholesterolemia as a worked example, and describes new public–private partnerships formed to commercialise genomic insights. It sets up Phase III (2025–2031), which aims to make whole-genome sequencing a standard part of the lifelong health record for 10% of Singapore's population. The paper positions Singapore, via NUS and its NPM partners (A*STAR, NUS Medicine, SingHealth and others), as a model for how small, ethnically diverse nations can contribute to global precision-medicine research.

Sources: Nature Genetics

3. "A Life Shaped by Learning": late geography teacher's bequest funds a new NUS undergraduate bursary

NUS Giving profiled the late Mr Tan Peng Hock (Arts and Social Sciences '69), who studied geography at NUS's old Bukit Timah campus before spending decades teaching the subject at Singapore schools including St Joseph's Institution. When he passed away in October 2025, former students turned up at his funeral — some straight from the airport — to describe how he had pushed them to keep studying and treated them with warmth despite a stern exterior. Before his death he left a bequest to NUS through his estate; his wife Mdm Chua Gek Choo and brother-in-law/executor Mr John Chua said he was explicit that he wanted the money to support NUS students because he believed everyone deserved a chance at education. The resulting Tan Peng Hock Bursary will provide financial support for NUS undergraduates going forward.

Sources: NUS Giving

4. "Growing Beyond Adversity": bursary-backed NUS Psychology grad now serves as a Singapore Police Force inspector

NUS Giving published the story of Jasmin Kaur Sandhu (Arts and Social Sciences '25), who navigated family violence and estrangement through much of her undergraduate years and, without family support or access to an education loan, feared she would have to drop out. A donor-funded bursary covered her tuition and, she said, "lifted the invisible weight I carried daily," letting her maintain a 4.0 GPA while working part-time jobs. With that stability she became a peer mentor in NUS's Teach SG programme, joined the cross-cultural Faithfully ASEAN youth initiative, and attended a University of Tokyo summer programme. She has since joined the Singapore Police Force as an inspector and says she hopes eventually to start a community non-profit for people in crisis.

Sources: NUS Giving

5. NUS South Asia scholar's analysis of India's "Cockroach" protests puts an NUS byline behind a major regional story

Dr Ronojoy Sen of the NUS Institute of South Asian Studies (ISAS) published a 28 July brief on the resignation of India's Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan, forced out by weeks of youth-led "Cockroach Janta Party" protests over mishandling of India's main medical-college entrance exam. Sen frames it as the first cabinet resignation of Narendra Modi's tenure driven by public pressure, argues the protests succeeded because they cut across India's usual political divides and went viral online, and notes the government has since introduced a bill with 10-year jail terms for exam-leak offences and formed a Nandan Nilekani-led reform task force. The piece is the kind of fast-turnaround policy commentary that keeps NUS-affiliated think tanks like ISAS in the regional news cycle, this time on a story that also made international outlets like Al Jazeera, CBS and NPR.

Sources: ISAS, NUS, Al Jazeera
